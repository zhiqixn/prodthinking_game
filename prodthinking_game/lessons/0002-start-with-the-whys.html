Symptom first, cause second

In Lesson 01 you learned to write down what goes wrong. That is usually a symptom: the part people can see. IDG’s second module adds one step before you look for solutions: understand why the problem exists. Its tool is the Five Whys, which moves you from symptom to cause.

Source: IDG, Start With The Whys.

The method is simple. State the problem, ask “why?”, then ask “why?” about the answer, and keep going.

The technique comes from Toyota’s production system. Here is Taiichi Ohno’s classic example, about a machine that stopped:

Source: Lean Enterprise Institute, 5 Whys, citing Ohno (1988).
  1. SymptomThe machine stopped.
  2. Why 1An overload blew the fuse.
  3. Why 2The bearing wasn’t lubricated enough.
  4. Why 3The lubrication pump wasn’t pumping enough.
  5. Why 4The pump shaft was worn and rattling.
  6. Why 5 · root causeThere was no strainer, so metal scraps got in.

Replace the fuse and the machine runs today. It will stop again, because the scraps are still getting in. Fit a strainer and the failure stops coming back.

Practice 1: Build the chain

Put the causes in order, starting with the one nearest the symptom. Click a cause to place it, and click a placed step to take it back out. The example is invented.

SymptomSmall businesses wait about eight weeks for a grant decision.

    This chain stops at four whys. Five is a rule of thumb: keep asking until you reach a cause you can remove.

    Choosing the right level to fix

    Two cautions from IDG. First, problems rarely have a single root cause: one symptom can branch into several chains. Second, the deepest cause isn’t always the one to fix. Pick a cause that passes two tests:

    1. It is within your control.
    2. It is closely connected to the outcome you want to change.
    Source: IDG, Start With The Whys, section “Choosing the right level to fix.”

    Ask “why?” far enough and you reach causes like “the economy” or “people are busy”. They are true, but you can’t act on them. Stop asking too early and you end up replacing the fuse.

    Practice 2: Pick the level

    You are on the agency’s digital team. For each chain, pick the cause to work on, using the two tests.

    Level to fix

    Symptom: elderly residents miss clinic appointments. You found these causes. Which do you work on?

    When letters are posted is in your agency’s hands and directly affects missed appointments. Postal speed and population ageing are outside your control. “Residents ignore letters” blames people, and asking why again leads back to the late letters.

    Symptom: grant decisions take eight weeks (the chain from Practice 1). Which cause do you work on?

    Moving the checklist before submission is within the team’s control, and it leads straight to fewer send-backs and faster decisions. The others are symptoms, or outside your reach.

    Practice 3: Your own chain

    IDG’s exercise is to take a real problem and ask why five times. Use one from your work, or try “Officers spend two hours a day re-typing PDF forms.” Keep asking until you reach something you can fix.

    Recall

    What does IDG say the Five Whys is for?

    Symptom to cause, so you understand why a problem exists before you pick a solution.

    Which two tests does IDG give for choosing the level to fix?

    Within your control, and closely connected to the outcome you want to change. The deepest cause isn’t automatically the right one.

    In Ohno’s example, you stop at “the fuse blew” and replace the fuse. What happens?

    The scraps keep getting in, so the failure comes back. Fixing the symptom isn’t fixing the problem, which is Lesson 01’s point again.

    For your game

    A digging mechanic

    A why-chain plays naturally as a descent. The player starts with a symptom card and digs one “why?” at a time, and some cards are red herrings. The real decision is where to stop: too shallow and the problem comes back next round, too deep and it’s out of the player’s control. Score the stop with IDG’s two tests.

    Questions to think about: how does the player find out a cause was a red herring? Does stopping too shallow cost something visible, like the same symptom returning?
