Product Thinking · Lesson 01 of the IDG pathway

Problems before solutions

Many project briefs arrive as a solution: “we need a chatbot.” Your first product-thinking skill is to spot that, and turn it back into a problem.

About 10 minutes · 2 exercises · 3 recall questions

Why this comes first

The Institute of Digital Government (IDG) opens its Product Thinking pathway with one claim. A project can be executed well and still struggle, because it solved the wrong problem. The fix is to diagnose the real problem first, and only then decide what to build.

Source: IDG Module 1, Understanding the Problem.

The module names three principles that the rest of the pathway builds on. This lesson covers the first.

  • Focus on the problemthis lesson
  • Chase outcomesMetrics module
  • Integrate policy, operations and technologylater
IDG shows the three principles as a graphic in Module 1. Their full treatment comes in later modules.

IDG also points out that many successful products, such as Grab, Amazon and Netflix, started by solving one specific problem for a defined group of users, and grew from there.

Spot the solution in disguise

A solution in disguise names the thing to build (a chatbot, a dashboard, an app) without saying what is going wrong, or for whom. It skips the diagnosis. If it ships, nobody can say whether it worked, because nothing defined what “worked” would mean.

A problem statement leaves the fix open and answers three questions:

  1. Who is affected?
  2. What goes wrong for them?
  3. How do you know? What evidence, and how big is it?
This three-question check is a simplified starting point for this lesson. IDG’s full version is the 4Cs Framework: Clarity, Consequence, Cause, Confirmation. See Craft a Clear Problem Statement.
Solution in disguise

We need a chatbot for the appointment booking page.

Problem

Residents booking online can’t find a free slot, so many call the hotline instead. About a third of hotline calls are about slots.

The figure in the problem example is invented to show the shape. In real work, it is the thing you go and check.

Practice 1: Problem or solution?

Sort each statement. You get feedback straight away. All examples are invented for practice.

Sorter

“We need a chatbot on the appointment booking page.”

It names something to build. It says nothing about who is struggling, or with what.

“First-time applicants often submit forms without the required documents, so their cases bounce back and take twice as long.”

Who: first-time applicants. What goes wrong: missing documents, so cases bounce. It also names a consequence you can measure.

“Officers spend about two hours a day re-typing data from PDF forms into the case system.”

Who and what, with a figure you could verify. It doesn’t say what to build, so the options stay open.

“Build a dashboard so managers can see the case backlog.”

A dashboard is a thing to build. The backlog might be the real problem, but this sentence skips straight past it.

“Elderly residents miss clinic appointments because reminder letters arrive after the appointment date.”

Who, what, and even a likely cause. Checking whether that cause is the real one is the subject of the next lesson.

“Let’s add AI to the grants portal.”

Technology first. Nothing here says what is wrong with the portal today.

“We should launch a mobile app for the scheme.”

An app is a channel. Who can’t use the scheme today, and why?

Practice 2: Turn it back into a problem

Your director forwards a one-line request. Rewrite it as a problem statement. You may invent plausible facts, but mark them as assumptions to check.

The automatic check only catches named solutions. Judge the rest with the checklist.
Model answer and checklist
Officers handling public feedback can’t keep up: around 2,000 emails arrive each month, and replies now take three weeks against a one-week target. (Figures are assumptions to verify.)
  • Says who is affected
  • Says what goes wrong for them
  • Says how you know, with evidence or a size
  • Names no solution, so AI stays one option among several

Recall

Answer from memory, without scrolling up. Recalling it now helps you remember it next week.

Recall

In IDG’s Module 1, why can a well-executed project still struggle?

Good execution can’t rescue the wrong problem. That is why diagnosis comes first.

Which of these is one of Module 1’s three principles?

The three are: focus on the problem, chase outcomes, and integrate policy, operations and technology.

What does a problem statement say that a solution in disguise usually leaves out?

Who, what goes wrong, and how you know. The other three are all decisions about a solution.

For your game

Is this a mechanic?

The sorter above could already be a round in the Product Thinking Game: a statement appears, and the player calls it problem or pitch. As a quiz, it gets dull fast. What could turn it into a game? Some starting ideas:

  • A stakeholder character who keeps pushing a favourite solution
  • Bonus points for rewriting a pitch into a problem, not just spotting it
  • A timer, or a queue of requests that fills up

Write down one idea. We’ll design with it after the next lesson.
